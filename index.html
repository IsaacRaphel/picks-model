About me

Economics student who likes testing ideas against real data.

I'm Isaac Raphel, an Economics major with a Business minor at Indiana University. I'm interested in how markets turn information into prices, and whether a careful, rules-based process can spot the places where they get it wrong.

Sports markets are a good place to study that. Every game has a clear price before it starts and a clear result after, thousands of games happen each season, and the feedback comes in overnight instead of over years. This project is how I'm learning forecasting, data pipelines and model evaluation by actually building one.

How it works

A daily pipeline: collect, predict, grade, learn.

I designed the system and its rules, and it runs on AI agents and scheduled jobs that do the daily data work. Every step is logged to structured datasets, so every prediction can be checked later against what actually happened.

01 · Morning

Collect the data

Pulls spreads, moneylines and prediction-market prices for up to 60 games, plus injury reports, rest and travel, public betting splits and line movement.

ingest
02 · Morning

Make a forecast

Builds its own projected spread and win probability from team strength, home/road form and game context, then compares it to the market to find real disagreements.

predict
03 · Midday + pregame

Track the market

Re-checks lines and late news before tip-off, recording how prices moved so the model can be measured against the final market price.

monitor
04 · Overnight

Grade and learn

Scores every forecast, separates bad luck from real mistakes, and turns repeated mistakes into new rules to test.

evaluate

Built-in discipline

The model is told not to double count information the market has already priced in, like home field or a well-known injury. Its edge has to come from something the price doesn't reflect yet. When it disagrees with the market by a lot, that's treated as a warning to recheck, not a reason to be confident.

Measured the right way

Wins and losses take hundreds of games to mean anything, so the model also tracks closing line value: whether its number beat the market's final price. Weekly reviews break results down by edge size, favorites vs. underdogs, home vs. away and league to catch problems early.

How the model evolves

Mistakes become hypotheses. Only the ones that hold up survive.

Every night, losses are tagged by cause: a bad read, the market simply being right, or noise like a late fluke score. Noise is ignored. Repeated real mistakes become "lessons," and each lesson is treated as a hypothesis, not a rule. The model tags every prediction a lesson changes, so each one builds its own track record.

// how a lesson is judged, once it has enough evidence if (lesson.picks < 15) status = "Testing" else if (lesson.winRate >= 0.524) status = "Keep" // beats the break-even rate else if (lesson.winRate < 0.47) status = "Dropped" // kept on file so it isn't relearned else status = "Testing" // decide at 30 picks

Example from week one: the data showed that predictions where the model disagreed with the market by 3+ points did worse than ones with small disagreements. That became a new hypothesis to recheck big disagreements instead of trusting them, and it's now being tested on new games.

Roadmap

The plan

Early Nov 2026

Build the pipeline

Daily data collection, forecasts across four leagues, automatic grading, and a live dashboard.

November 2026

Collect data and test hypotheses

Grow the dataset to 1,000+ graded games while the lessons system adds, tests, keeps and drops rules.

End of November

Formula v1

Combine the lessons that held up into one written formula, with the evidence behind each piece.

December 2026

Out-of-sample test

Run v1 on new games without changing it. If it holds up against the market, it becomes the model. If not, revise and test v2.

This is a research project. Results are tracked with simulated stakes only, and early numbers are a small sample.
