Model vs. simple strategies

A forecast only matters if it beats something simpler. These are the same graded games, picked by rules anyone could follow: always take the favorite, always the underdog, always the home team, and the public rating system with no adjustments. Everything here is against the spread: "always favorite" means laying the points with the favorite every game, not just picking them to win.

Simple strategies are graded on each game's latest posted DraftKings spread; the model is graded on the line it actually picked. "Ratings only" starts once rating data is logged (from Nov 13).

Market efficiency

Is the betting market efficient?

Every moneyline is a price, and a price implies a probability. After removing the sportsbook's cut, this compares what DraftKings' prices implied with how often those teams actually won. If the market is efficient, the dots sit on the dashed line.

  • Prices as information. A competitive market's price should reflect all public information, so a forecast only has value where it knows something the price doesn't. That's why the model is built to avoid double counting what's already priced in.
  • The vig is a wedge. Implied probabilities on both sides add up to more than 100%. The extra is the book's cut, which works like a tax wedge between what bettors pay and the fair price, and it's why break-even is 52.4% instead of 50%.
  • Risk preferences. A well-known pattern in betting markets is the favorite-longshot bias: long shots tend to win less often than their prices imply, because some bettors pay extra for a small chance at a big payout, the same way people buy lottery tickets.
