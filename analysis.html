Calibration

When the model says a team wins 70% of the time, does it? Points on the dashed line mean the probabilities are honest; above it means the model was too cautious, below it too confident.

Market efficiency

Is the betting market efficient?

Every moneyline is a price, and a price implies a probability. After removing the sportsbook's cut, this compares what DraftKings' prices implied with how often those teams actually won. If the market is efficient, the dots sit on the dashed line.

  • Prices as information. A competitive market's price should reflect all public information, so a forecast only has value where it knows something the price doesn't. That's why the model is built to avoid double counting what's already priced in.
  • The vig is a wedge. Implied probabilities on both sides add up to more than 100%. The extra is the book's cut, which works like a tax wedge between what bettors pay and the fair price, and it's why break-even is 52.4% instead of 50%.
  • Risk preferences. A well-known pattern in betting markets is the favorite-longshot bias: long shots tend to win less often than their prices imply, because some bettors pay extra for a small chance at a big payout, the same way people buy lottery tickets.

Intermediate microeconomics

The sportsbook as a firm

A sportsbook is a firm selling a product (a bet) at a price (the line and the odds). The data the model collects every day doubles as a dataset on how that market works. These charts test a few textbook ideas against it.

Law of one price

Do competing books charge the same price?

The same game's spread at DraftKings vs. FanDuel. In a competitive market with easy comparison shopping, identical products should sell at one price, so most gaps should be zero.

Markups

How big is the book's markup?

The hold is the share of every dollar bet the book expects to keep if action is balanced. It's the price above "cost" (the fair odds), like a markup in a market with a few big sellers.

Supply and demand

Does the price move when demand piles onto one side?

Each dot is a game. Across: the share of bets on the more popular side. Up: how many points the spread moved against that side from open to the latest line (positive means the book made the popular side more expensive). If books just balanced demand, dots would climb to the right.

Asymmetric information

The crowd vs. the bigger bettors

Ticket counts show where the crowd is. When a side's share of money is clearly higher than its share of bets, fewer, larger bettors are on it, often a sign of better-informed money. Which side covers more?

Strategic behavior

Model with vs. against the crowd

If prices are set knowing how the public will bet, the best response can be to go the other way. This splits the model's spread picks by whether they sided with the majority of bets.
